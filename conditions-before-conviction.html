Conditions Before Conviction

How We Become Certain—and What Our Certainty Changes

We rarely experience the conditions that shape a judgment. We experience the conclusion.

Conditions Before Conviction examines what happens before an explanation becomes convincing enough to rely on—and what changes once we begin acting from it.

Where it starts

  • A partner becomes distant.
  • A child behaves in a way we do not understand.
  • A colleague does something that seems revealing.
  • A decision suddenly feels obvious.
  • A conclusion about ourselves begins sounding like fact.

By then, we usually have an explanation.
It may be right.

But another question remains: how did this become so convincing to me?

The central idea

What conviction means here

Conviction, in this book, does not mean dogmatism. It is the point at which a conclusion has acquired enough authority for us to rely on it. Certainty is the confidence we experience around that authority.

This book does not assume the initial conclusion is wrong. A conclusion may survive examination and become stronger. The aim is not less certainty. It is a more responsible relationship with the authority certainty carries.

What happened before this became convincing enough for me to rely on?
What will this conviction change once I act from it?

Conditions Before Conviction enters established conversations about judgment, certainty, interpretation, and belief, but follows this specific question upstream and downstream: what made a conclusion convincing, and what begins to change once we rely on it.

What the book is trying to do

The objective is not less certainty. Conditions Before Conviction aims for judgments that remain answerable to evidence, context, consequence, and revision—strong enough to guide real action, and honest enough to change when they should.

From the opening chapter

True is not the same as complete

The explanations behind our convictions are often not ridiculous. They may be true.

  • I like helping people.
  • My child needs protection.
  • This employee has struggled.
  • Something in this relationship has changed.

Sometimes the problem is not that an explanation is false. It is that it is true enough to stop the inquiry.

Before conviction

Not everything that makes a conclusion convincing is evidence for it.

History, identity, expectation, relationship, fear, fatigue, social cost, desire, and imagined futures may all participate in how convincing a conclusion feels—without automatically making that conclusion wrong.

The purpose of examining this is not generosity. It is accuracy. Understanding what shaped a conviction does not eliminate accountability for it. Context should make accountability more precise, not weaker.

The downstream question

What our certainty changes

A conviction eventually does something. It changes what we ask, what we ignore, whom we trust, what we permit, what we refuse, and what actions begin to feel reasonable.

Sometimes acting from a conviction also changes the conditions under which the next evidence appears. The moment we act from a conviction, that conviction becomes one of the conditions in the situation it was trying to explain.

Conditions Before Conviction stays with the harder question this raises: if an earlier conviction helped shape the conditions under which later evidence appeared, what should that do to the authority we give that evidence—and to the conviction itself?

Where the question came from

Validation became personal before it became explicit.

Looking back, I can see a developmental line. More than two decades spent close to consequential human judgment—assessment, leadership development, facilitated dialogue—kept returning me to the same question: what is shaping the conclusion we are seeing here?

Part of the question grew somewhere else entirely. I once pursued an ambitious technical venture, Banna-Venturi, and for a period became genuinely absorbed by the possibility that it could generate extraordinary success. That imagined future became psychologically vivid. Sometimes what makes a conviction difficult to examine is not only the evidence supporting it, but the future that depends on it being true.

The venture eventually met failed validation. The lesson was not that failure produced success. It intensified a different interest: what evidence actually permits us to claim, what conditions must be valid before something consequential proceeds, and the difference between an output existing and that output deserving authority. That question later contributed to Phys-Valid, a technical validation and governance system—an engineering answer to an upstream question.

Conditions Before Conviction asks what a related question looks like in human judgment, where evidence is incomplete, meaning is contextual, and people cannot be reduced to engineering rules.

Who this is for

  • Someone trying to understand another person
  • A parent, or a partner
  • Someone making a consequential judgment
  • Someone carrying a conclusion about themselves
  • Someone who has watched a thing feel obvious, then grow more complicated

Leaders, educators, coaches, consultants, assessors, and other people professionals will find the ideas directly useful in their work—but the book begins with the human situation, not the profession.
